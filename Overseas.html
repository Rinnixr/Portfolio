Overview

Overseas was a top-down endless survival fishing game developed for GAM150 - Software Engineering Project 2 during my first year at DigiPen Singapore.

The player controls a fishing boat navigating a dangerous ocean while managing health, supplies, finances, and an increasingly demanding quota. Temporary fishing spots appear around the map, forcing players to decide when to fish, when to avoid danger, and how to spend their earnings before the next deadline arrives.

There is no traditional win condition. Instead, the game continues for as long as the player can survive and meet an exponentially increasing financial quota. Failing to earn the required amount before the timer expires immediately ends the run.

I worked as the project's Gameplay Programmer / UI & Art Lead. On the programming side, I implemented the player's boat movement and the complete quota and deadline system. On the visual side, I established the game's pixel-art direction, defined asset guidelines, designed the complete UI/UX, produced the majority of the game's 2D assets, and integrated the visual feedback needed to communicate the game's systems clearly.

Project Design Goals

  • Create an endless survival loop built around fishing, exploration, and escalating financial pressure.
  • Make each run increasingly difficult through a quota system rather than traditional level progression.
  • Encourage players to balance risk, time, resources, and income.
  • Give boat movement a sense of momentum and inertia rather than using standard character movement.
  • Use temporary fishing locations to encourage movement across the map.
  • Keep fishing simple enough to understand quickly while still requiring player timing.
  • Establish a cohesive, subdued pixel-art visual direction centred around nostalgia.
  • Communicate health, quotas, deadlines, fishing, upgrades, and other systems clearly through the UI.
  • Build the game primarily through C++ systems on DigiPen's minimal Alpha Engine framework.

Core Gameplay Loop

Overseas was structured around a repeating survival loop. Players explored the ocean looking for temporary fishing spots, which appeared for a limited amount of time before disappearing.

Successfully catching fish allowed the player to earn money. That income contributed toward the current quota and could also be spent on supplies, upgrades, and cosmetic options. At the end of each quota period, the game checked whether the required amount had been earned.

If the player succeeded, they received:

  • A new, larger quota
  • A new time limit
  • Another survival cycle

If the quota was not met before the deadline, the run ended immediately. The required amount increased exponentially as the game continued, placing greater pressure on the player to fish efficiently, avoid damage, and make better purchasing decisions. Because there was no final victory condition, the goal was simply to survive for as long as possible.

Player Movement & Boat Handling

I programmed the player's movement system in C++. The initial conventional WASD implementation used direct directional control, but playtesting showed that it felt too similar to controlling a character on land.

I reworked the system around acceleration, deceleration, steering, and drag:

  • W - accelerate
  • S - decelerate
  • A / D - steer
  • Momentum and drag to control continued movement

Instead of instantly changing direction, the boat needed time to accelerate, slow down, and turn. This gave movement more weight and made navigation around enemies and hazards more deliberate. The change directly resulted from playtesting and improved the feel of the system rather than simply its numerical values.

Quota & Deadline System

I designed and programmed the game's quota tracking, timer, deadline, fail-state logic, and associated UI integration. At the beginning of a survival cycle, players were assigned a target amount of money to earn within a defined period.

The system tracked:

  • Current earnings
  • Required quota
  • Remaining time
  • Quota completion
  • Deadline failure
  • Progression into the next quota cycle

Successfully meeting the target advanced the player into another cycle with a higher requirement and a new deadline. If the timer reached zero first, the system triggered the fail state and ended the run. I connected the system to the HUD so players could continuously understand their progress.

Fishing System

Fishing spots spawned temporarily around the ocean and disappeared after a limited period. This created a time-sensitive objective within the larger quota timer: players had to find available fishing areas, reach them safely, complete the mini-game, and continue moving before opportunities disappeared.

The fishing interaction used a timing-based mini-game; players pressed the input at the correct moment to hit the appropriate section of a moving bar. Another team member programmed the mechanic itself, while I produced the visual assets and animations used by the mini-game.

Survival & Hazards

The ocean contained several threats that could damage the player's boat:

  • Hostile sea creatures
  • Environmental hazards
  • Physical collisions
  • Passive damage over time

Enemies included large serpent-like creatures and another creature visually resembling an aquatic sandworm. Players could purchase and use supplies to repair the boat, creating a direct trade-off between survival spending and quota progression.

Economy, Upgrades & Progression

Money served multiple purposes throughout a run. Players needed enough income to satisfy the active quota, but that same resource could improve their chances of surviving future cycles.

Purchasable content included:

  • Gameplay upgrades
  • Repair-related supplies
  • Cosmetic skins
  • Other progression options

This created a recurring trade-off between short-term survival, long-term improvement, and meeting the deadline. Spending aggressively could strengthen the player but make the current quota harder to reach; saving everything could leave the player less prepared for increasingly dangerous cycles.

File I/O & Persistence

The project included File I/O to persist information between play sessions, including:

  • Player progression
  • Purchased upgrades
  • Settings

This allowed progression and preferences to persist rather than resetting whenever the application was closed. Persistence was also part of the project's software-engineering requirements, connecting gameplay state with data that could be reliably written and restored.

UI / UX Design

I designed the entire UI/UX system for Overseas and produced its GUI assets. The interface needed to continuously communicate multiple survival systems without obscuring the play area.

My work included:

  • HUD design and health information
  • Quota tracking, deadline, and timer feedback
  • Fishing mini-game, shop, and upgrade interfaces
  • Menus and settings interfaces
  • Progression and game-state feedback
  • GUI assets and icons

Because quota and time pressure were central to the game, the interface made the player's financial situation immediately understandable. I integrated the UI feedback directly with my quota, deadline, and fail-state systems.

Art Direction & Visual Development

I served as the project's UI & Art Lead and established the overall visual direction. The concept began from the team's shared interest in fishing and its nostalgic associations. We chose pixel art because its retro visual language reinforced that feeling.

Rather than highly saturated colours, I established a desaturated visual approach suited to the ocean setting and survival tone. I defined sprite-resolution standards, asset guidelines, the general pixel-art approach, UI visual language, animation presentation, and overall visual consistency.

Asset Production

All visual assets used in Overseas were created specifically for the project rather than taken from external asset libraries. I created the majority of the game's 2D pixel-art assets, including fish sprites, GUI assets, HUD elements, icons, fishing mini-game assets, animation states, mini-game animations, and supporting visual assets.

The primary player boat and monster assets were produced by another team member, who modelled and animated them in Maya and Blender before rendering the results into spritesheets. This preserved the sprite-based presentation while reducing the amount of frame-by-frame drawing required for larger animated objects.

Animation & Visual Feedback

I created the animation states and much of the 2D animated feedback used throughout the game, including the fishing mini-game and other GUI-driven gameplay states. Because the game depended heavily on timers, quotas, and short interactions, animation also served a usability purpose: it made changes in game state clear without requiring the player to stop navigating the ocean.

Working with Alpha Engine

The project was developed using Alpha Engine, the C++ framework provided by DigiPen. The engine supplied a basic foundation and documentation but not a complete editor or high-level interface comparable to Unity.

Many systems normally provided by an engine or UI framework therefore had to be developed by the team, including:

  • Button interaction
  • Collision handling
  • Input behaviour
  • Interface logic
  • Gameplay-system integration
  • State management

For my own work, this meant implementing the player and quota loop while connecting those systems to the interfaces and visual feedback I designed.

Team Structure

The five-person team's primary ownership was divided between:

  • Monster AI
  • Overall game structure
  • Shop structure
  • Fishing mini-game
  • UI / art / quota systems

My primary responsibilities were the player, quota system, UI/UX, visual direction, and asset production. This structure allowed members to concentrate on a major gameplay area while integrating those systems into the same survival loop.

Playtesting & Iteration

We conducted playtests throughout development to evaluate gameplay readability and feel. The original conventional directional WASD movement was functional but did not communicate the feeling of piloting a boat. Based on testing, I redesigned it around acceleration, deceleration, steering, drag, and retained momentum. The revised movement required players to anticipate turns and slow down before hazards, better supporting the intended nautical feel and making navigation more meaningful.

Production Constraints

The largest constraint on Overseas was development time. The project ran alongside a heavy DigiPen course load with multiple concurrent high-credit modules, so scope and implementation had to be carefully prioritised.

For my responsibilities, this meant balancing:

  • Gameplay programming
  • Complete UI/UX design and implementation
  • Production of the majority of the game's 2D artwork

Maintaining a consistent asset pipeline while implementing gameplay required prioritising assets and interfaces that directly affected clarity before lower-priority visual polish.

Project Outcome

Overseas received an A grade for GAM150 - Software Engineering Project 2.

The project combined software-engineering requirements with game production: building gameplay systems in C++, working within a lightweight engine framework, implementing persistent data, and integrating independently developed systems into a complete game.

For me, it combined gameplay programming, UI/UX design, and visual direction, and provided an opportunity to build both the internal logic and player-facing presentation of the quota, deadline, HUD, and fail-state loop.

The main inspiration for our game concept came from the simple fact that most of our members enjoyed fishing. It was something nostalgic and memorable for them.

With that, we identified pixel art as the artstyle that resonated with us the most. The retro-vibes aligned with our main concept - Nostalgia!

For this project, I led the asset creation, creating the bulk of assets, created our overall colour palette and designed the UIUX of the game.
Aside from that, I coded the player and created the daily quota and deadline.
