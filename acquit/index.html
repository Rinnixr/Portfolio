Overview

Acquit was a single-player courtroom strategy card game developed for Introduction to Game Design during my first year at DigiPen Singapore.

Players take on the role of a defence attorney preparing for and arguing a court case. The game is divided into two phases:

  • Pre-Trial, where the player investigates and builds a hand of Evidence and Tactics.
  • Trial, where those resources are committed against a sequence of Arguments and used to modify D20 rolls.

The project was based thematically on the O.J. Simpson murder trial as part of the module's "based on a true story" requirement. Rather than attempting to reproduce courtroom procedure exactly, we used details from the case as the foundation for a strategic game about preparation, risk management, and argument resolution.

The finished game contained approximately 40 cards across Investigation, Evidence, Tactic, Motion, and Argument card types.

I worked as the project's Game Designer and Art Lead. Another teammate and I handled most of the detailed mechanics design, while I took primary responsibility for the numerical structure that connected the systems together.

I designed the D20 resolution system, determined card draw and distribution, established the victory threshold, balanced every Argument Difficulty Class, determined Evidence and Tactic limits, contributed to set bonuses, and co-designed the Motion system.

I also drove physical prototyping and playtesting, designed every card template, created approximately 85% of the final artwork, wrote and designed the rulebook, and prepared the finished Print and Play package.

The project received an A-, the highest grade in the class.

The die needed to matter, but preparation needed to matter more.

Design Goals

  • Translate preparing and arguing a legal case into an understandable strategy game.
  • Make Pre-Trial decisions meaningfully affect Trial outcomes.
  • Use randomness without making success feel arbitrary.
  • Give Evidence different levels of strength and flexibility so hand-building involved evaluation rather than simple collection.
  • Support different risk preferences through Motion selection.
  • Make limited-use resources create meaningful timing decisions.
  • Reward players for recognizing useful card combinations.
  • Use historical case details as thematic material without allowing strict realism to make the game cumbersome.
  • Produce a ruleset clear enough for players without tabletop experience to learn independently.

Designing Around Preparation

The core design question behind Acquit was:

How much control should preparation give the player over an uncertain outcome?

If success depended almost entirely on the D20, there would be little reason to carefully build a hand during Pre-Trial. If Evidence bonuses were strong enough to guarantee success, the die would become meaningless.

The game therefore needed to sit between those extremes.

The final structure became:

Investigate → Evaluate Evidence → Build Hand → Select Motion → Counter Arguments → Manage Resources → Score Persuasion

The player spends the first phase improving the quality of the tools they will have later. Once Trial begins, those preparation choices become constraints.

The player cannot simply search for the perfect answer anymore. They have to decide how to use the Evidence and Tactics they already collected.

This allowed preparation to improve probability without completely removing uncertainty.

Pre-Trial & Hand Building

Pre-Trial lasts five turns, corresponding to the game's five Investigation cards.

Rather than giving the player a fixed starting hand, Investigation cards allow them to manipulate the Discovery Deck through effects such as drawing, peeking, keeping, discarding, and shuffling.

The final Print and Play set includes Investigation cards such as Private Investigator, Paper Trail, Police Report, Surveillance Footage, and Anonymous Tip.

The goal was to make this phase about evaluating future usefulness, not simply gathering the largest number of cards.

Players needed to consider how strong an Evidence modifier was, how many Arguments it could counter, whether it was highly specialized, whether a weaker card had broader usefulness, whether a Tactic could make another card more valuable, and which Motion they intended to take into Trial.

A typical hand reached approximately 15 cards, with the exact structure affected by the player's choices and Motion.

This created the first major strategic trade-off. A powerful Evidence card might be extremely valuable against one specific Argument but useless elsewhere. A more generic card might provide a smaller modifier while remaining relevant across several possible Arguments.

The player therefore had to choose between specialization and flexibility.

Evidence as Strategic Value

Evidence cards were designed around two main variables: strength and applicability.

Some Evidence provided larger numerical bonuses but could only counter a narrow group of Arguments. Others provided smaller modifiers but remained relevant in more situations.

The final Print and Play set contains Evidence with varying bonuses and compatibility across different Arguments.

I balanced these relationships so the strongest numerical card was not automatically the most useful card. The value of Evidence depended on the rest of the player's hand and what appeared during Trial.

Evidence was also discarded after use unless recovered through another effect. This made commitment important.

Playing a strong Evidence card now improved the chance of winning the current Argument, but removed that resource from future decisions.

Tactics & Resource Timing

Tactic cards provided exceptions to the normal rules.

They could recover previously used cards, allow rerolls, modify Argument difficulty, increase scoring, allow additional Evidence, or change how a specific Trial interaction resolved.

The final game included cards such as Expert Witness, Objection, Surprise Witness, Re-frame Argument, and Cross-Examination.

Most Tactics were single-use unless another effect recovered them.

I also determined how many Evidence and Tactic cards players could normally commit to a single Argument. These restrictions were necessary because unlimited stacking would allow players to eliminate uncertainty completely.

The limit created a resource-timing problem instead:

Is this Argument important enough to spend one of my strongest tools on?

That decision became more meaningful as the Trial progressed and the player's hand became smaller.

Motion System & Playstyle

Before entering Trial, the player chooses one Motion that remains active for the entire phase.

The Motion system was co-designed by another designer and me. The final game included Suppress, Dismiss, and Continuance.

Each altered the player's strategy in a different way.

I wanted Motion selection to establish the player's broader risk preference before Trial began. One approach might offer stronger rewards at greater risk. Another might favour consistency. Another could provide additional protection against poor outcomes.

Early versions had a significant balance problem because one Motion was clearly stronger than the others. This also caused the game to favour a more aggressive playstyle.

Through repeated testing, I adjusted the numerical relationships so Motion choice became a strategic preference rather than a solved decision.

The goal was not to make every Motion identical in value. The goal was to make each one support a different viable way of approaching the Trial.

Trial & D20 Resolution

I designed the game's D20 resolution system after being inspired by probability checks while playing Baldur's Gate 3.

I liked the way a roll could remain uncertain while character preparation and modifiers still gave the player meaningful influence over the result.

I adapted that idea into:

D20 + Evidence + Tactics + Motion ≥ Argument Difficulty Class

During Trial, Arguments are revealed individually. The player evaluates the Argument, decides whether their compatible Evidence is worth committing, chooses whether to spend any Tactics, and then rolls.

The official rulebook presents this sequence through the Trial setup and resolution flow.

This structure connected all the game's systems together. Pre-Trial determined which cards were available. Evidence determined numerical preparation. Tactics allowed situational rule manipulation. Motions changed the player's wider strategy. Argument DC created the challenge. The D20 introduced uncertainty into the result.

The die therefore mattered, but it was surrounded by systems that allowed the player to influence their odds.

Argument Difficulty & Risk

I balanced the Difficulty Class of every Argument card.

Early versions exposed several problems. Some Arguments were so easy that the player had little reason to spend Evidence. Others were so difficult that winning them depended too heavily on having very specific card combinations.

This weakened the strategic value of hand-building.

I adjusted Argument difficulty in relation to available Evidence, Evidence modifiers, Evidence specificity, Tactic availability, Persuasion reward, set membership, and Motion effects.

This was important because Difficulty Class could not be balanced in isolation. A difficult Argument could be reasonable if it offered significant Persuasion or contributed toward a valuable set. A low-value Argument needed to be achievable without requiring the player's strongest resources.

Scoring & Set Bonuses

The player earns Persuasion by successfully countering Arguments.

I designed the overall scoring structure and final victory requirement. The final rulebook requires the player to reach 20 or more Persuasion to win.

Arguments could also belong to sets. Completing a set provided an additional score bonus, with more difficult sets offering greater value.

One of our early balancing problems was that set completion became too important. Players could struggle to reach the victory threshold unless they completed a set, which made individual Argument victories feel less meaningful.

We adjusted the numbers so sets became valuable opportunities rather than mandatory objectives. A player could pursue a set if their hand supported it, but other scoring strategies remained viable.

Playtesting & Numerical Balancing

We conducted more than 30 rounds of playtesting.

Testing involved team members, friends, family, outside players, experienced players, and people with little or no gaming experience.

I handled the majority of the game's numerical balancing. Rather than changing values after every isolated result, I generally looked for patterns across approximately five games before making adjustments.

This helped distinguish genuine system problems from unusually lucky or unlucky rolls.

The main problems identified during testing included Arguments being too easy, other Arguments being disproportionately difficult, sets being too difficult to complete, set bonuses becoming too important to winning, one Motion being significantly stronger, aggressive play being more effective than cautious play, and Evidence distribution producing inconsistent hands.

We did not remove any core mechanics.

Instead, we repeatedly adjusted the relationships between:

Draw Rate → Hand Composition → Evidence Strength → Evidence Applicability → Argument DC → Motion → Persuasion → Set Bonus → Victory Threshold

This was one of the project's most valuable design lessons. A small change to one value could significantly affect how players evaluated several other systems.

Art Direction & Card UI

I served as Art Lead and produced approximately 85% of the final visual assets.

The visual direction drew from courtroom interiors, wooden courtroom architecture, paper case files, evidence folders, legal documents, stamps, institutional seals, and typewritten records.

I wanted the game to feel like the player was assembling and presenting a case rather than simply holding a deck of generic strategy cards.

The final visual language used cream and brown tones, paper textures, document-inspired layouts, simple iconography, and stamped labels. The Print and Play package applies this visual system consistently across each major card category.

No AI-generated artwork was used.

Two teammates contributed additional visual assets, while I handled the overall visual system and majority of production art.

Card Templates as UI Design

I designed every card template.

Because Acquit was a physical game, the cards themselves were the interface. There were no hover states, tooltips, or expandable menus.

Every card needed to communicate its purpose immediately through layout. The player needed to identify what card type they were holding, when it could be used, what bonus it provided, which Arguments it applied to, whether it was a one-time effect, how valuable an Argument was, and what information required immediate attention.

I treated information hierarchy as part of the game design rather than a purely visual concern.

Each card category needed to remain visually related to the rest of the game while still being recognizable at a glance during play.

Rulebook & Player Onboarding

I wrote and designed the complete rulebook.

The challenge was turning a game with several card types, two gameplay phases, D20 resolution, compatibility rules, and scoring into something a new player could understand without us explaining it.

The final rulebook separates play into Pre-Trial and Trial and uses diagrams to communicate card placement and table setup.

I also designed the table layouts and instructional graphics.

We tested the final documentation with players of different ages and levels of gaming familiarity. This included people with little gaming experience or interest.

They were able to read the rules, set up the game, understand the card categories, play through both phases, resolve Arguments, and calculate the final result without needing to ask us how the game worked.

For me, this was an important usability benchmark.

A physical game's rules are part of its interface. If players cannot learn the system independently, the design is not fully communicating itself.

Print & Play

I assembled the complete Print and Play package.

The final file contains the Investigation, Evidence, Tactic, Motion, and Argument cards alongside supporting printable components, including a D20 template.

Preparing the project for print required considering card dimensions, text readability, information density, template consistency, front and back alignment, cutting and assembly, table space, and physical handling.

This turned the project from a classroom prototype into a reproducible physical game that other players could assemble and play independently.

Project Outcome

Acquit received an A-, the highest grade in the class.

The final game contained approximately 40 cards, five functional card categories, a five-turn Pre-Trial phase, hand-building and card manipulation, D20 Argument resolution, Evidence matching, limited-use Tactics, three persistent Motion options, difficulty-based Arguments, Persuasion scoring, set bonuses, a 20-point victory requirement, a complete rulebook, and a full Print and Play package.

For me, the most important outcome was learning how to design around the relationship between randomness and control.

The D20 created uncertainty. Investigation gave the player control over preparation. Evidence changed probability. Tactics gave players situational flexibility. Motions supported different strategic attitudes. Scoring rewarded decisions about which opportunities were worth pursuing.

The final design relationship became:

Investigation → Hand Quality → Evidence Choice → Resource Commitment → Probability → Argument Result → Persuasion

Together, these systems created the intended experience: prepare intelligently, evaluate risk, and use limited resources to turn uncertain situations in your favour.
