Overview

The Unraveling was a narrative horror game developed for GAM250, Software Engineering Project 4 at DigiPen Singapore. It was the second half of our two-part Software Engineering Project sequence and the primary game built using NAgine, the custom engine we developed during GAM200.

The game follows a student investigating the disappearance of her best friend, Yuki, at the abandoned Silent Veil High School. Years earlier, a student named Kayo had been blamed without evidence for the disappearance of a Faculty Office key. Rumours escalated into bullying, Kayo disappeared, and the school became trapped in a supernatural loop. The player uncovers what happened to Yuki while learning the truth behind Kayo and the curse.

The experience combined stealth, puzzle horror, narrative exploration, and sanity management. Players moved through the school, solved puzzles tied to Kayo’s past, collected memorabilia, avoided Kayo, and managed sanity while progressing through the story.

I entered GAM250 intending to focus more heavily on technical development. However, the project began without a sufficiently finalized gameplay, puzzle, visual, and level-design foundation for programmers to build against. I stepped into the Design Lead role during the first week, consolidated the existing work, finalized direction, redesigned gameplay and progression where necessary, and established production priorities. Alongside design leadership, I worked as a Gameplay Programmer and produced approximately 90% of the final game’s artwork.

Project Design Goals

  • Build a complete game entirely inside our custom NAgine engine and use the GAM200 engine and editor as the game-production workflow.
  • Create a narrative horror experience focused on tension rather than constant enemy encounters.
  • Combine puzzles, environmental storytelling, stealth, and exploration in one progression structure.
  • Make sanity both the player’s health and a source of audiovisual horror feedback.
  • Use one enemy whose line-of-sight and sound detection created unpredictable pressure.
  • Alternate exploration, puzzle solving, scripted horror sequences, and chase encounters to maintain tension.
  • Use memorabilia and environmental details to communicate narrative beyond cutscenes.
  • Create an original pixel-art visual identity and restructure scope when the original design could no longer fit the schedule.

Narrative & Game Premise

Silent Veil High School was once prestigious, but became connected to Kayo’s disappearance after a Faculty Office key vanished before final examinations. A rumour blamed the isolated Film Club student despite no evidence. The key was eventually found, but Kayo had already disappeared; thereafter, anyone entering the school after dark risked becoming trapped in its supernatural loop.

After Yuki disappears while investigating the legend, the protagonist follows her trail, uncovers Kayo’s story, and learns what happened to those trapped by the school. The narrative explored rumour, guilt, obsession, collective blame, and the consequences of silence through interactable environments, memorabilia, discoveries, and contextual dialogue rather than relying only on cinematics.

Taking Over Design Leadership

I had intended to allow the existing design team to lead the creative development. At the start of the trimester, however, the Unity prototype from the previous phase remained largely placeholder-based; several puzzles were unfinished or unconfirmed, visual direction was incomplete, and gameplay and progression decisions were unresolved.

Without finalized puzzles, objectives, layouts, interactions, and asset requirements, implementation could not progress efficiently. I took over Design Lead responsibilities and spent the first week consolidating the project into a production-ready direction.

  • Finalized the game loop, four primary puzzles, player success and failure flows, objectives, and progression.
  • Designed enemy encounter pacing and restructured level progression.
  • Established visual priorities, player-flow documentation, design delegation, and implementation requirements.
  • Identified features that needed to be reduced or removed.

Scope Reduction & Production Recovery

The original design was more open, with freer school exploration and planned shifts between rundown and modern states. Maintaining two complete environmental presentations and their gameplay support was not sustainable within the remaining schedule.

I restructured the game into a more linear progression, allowing us to control routing, reduce edge cases, simplify objective tracking, and focus resources on the strongest parts of the experience. The state-change feature was removed, and I revised inherited layouts containing dead ends, unclear routes, or structures that did not support progression. This preserved the core experience while removing features that threatened completion.

Core Game Loop

Explore → Discover Clues → Solve Puzzles → Avoid Kayo → Manage Sanity → Progress the Narrative

Players searched the school for puzzle items, memorabilia, and clues while remaining aware of Kayo’s location. Puzzle completion rewarded progression and sanity recovery, while mistakes and encounters increased pressure. The loop was designed to alternate curiosity and vulnerability: exploration encouraged investigation but exposed players to danger.

Level Design & Progression

I designed the game’s overall level flow, player routing, puzzle progression, horror pacing, and encounter placement across hallways, the AV Room, Courtyard, Faculty Offices, Classroom 3B, and Music Room. Each space contributed to both gameplay and Kayo’s backstory.

The player first discovers a clue leading to the AV Room, where playing a VHS tape introduces Kayo and triggers the first major chase. The final linear structure maintained pacing control and prevented players from entering areas before required systems or narrative states were ready.

Puzzle Design

I finalized and designed the four main puzzle sequences used to structure progression:

  • Courtyard Well: find a rope and bucket, attach them to the well, and complete a timing interaction to retrieve a key. Failure creates additional danger and can trigger Kayo.
  • Faculty Office Picture: find missing photograph fragments and reconstruct the image, which provides narrative information and directs the player to Classroom 3B.
  • Classroom Arrangement: use a seating chart to physically reposition tables and chairs until the room matches the reference.
  • Music Room: collect and reconstruct broken sheet music, then use the completed score to play the correct piano melody and advance the final sequence.

The puzzles were not only obstacles: they delivered narrative information and directed movement through the school.

Sanity System

I programmed sanity as the game’s primary health and tension mechanic. It decreased passively and during Kayo encounters, while players recovered it by completing puzzles, progressing important objectives, and remaining near designated light sources.

Candles and lights acted as temporary safety and recovery zones, encouraging active movement. At zero sanity, the player failed and a dedicated death sequence played. Declining sanity also reduced visibility and increased visual distortion, using the game’s presentation to reinforce vulnerability rather than relying solely on a numerical HUD.

Enemy AI, Stealth & Chase Design

Kayo was the game’s single primary enemy. She could detect the player through line of sight, sound, player actions, and failed interactions or puzzles. Direct line of sight triggered a chase; players could break visual contact or hide in lockers and cabinets to escape.

Playtesting showed some interactions were overly punishing, so I reduced encounter frequency, gave Kayo acceleration rather than immediate full speed, increased player reaction time, improved hiding interactions, and added a line-of-sight search before she could discover a player inside a locker. This created a readable tension window and made discovery understandable.

Player Systems & Gameplay Programming

I programmed a substantial portion of player-facing gameplay in C# using NAgine’s scripting layer: player movement, sanity, journal, inventory, memorabilia collection, item equipping, item inspection, interaction logic, and supporting puzzle behaviour. This validated the GAM200 engine architecture: game-specific programming did not need to be hardcoded into the C++ engine.

Reusable Interactable Framework

I designed and programmed a reusable C# Interactable base class. Rather than writing separate interaction behaviour for every object, game objects could attach a Script component and inherit the common architecture. It supported doors, pickups, puzzle objects, narrative items, and environmental interactions, providing shared detection and player-facing messaging.

Our editor did not yet expose all interaction values directly through the Inspector, so individual behaviour still required C# configuration. Even so, the base class reduced duplicated code and created a consistent approach to interactable game objects.

Journal, Inventory & Memorabilia

I designed and implemented the journal, the central player interface. Its Inventory let players inspect, equip, and use gameplay items. Memorabilia were narrative collectibles that helped reconstruct Kayo’s story and retained discoveries so important information did not rely on remembering environmental interactions. The journal also consolidated volume controls and basic control information.

UI / UX Design

I designed the complete UI/UX and created its visual assets: main menu, journal, inventory, memorabilia interface, settings, interaction prompts, item inspection, gameplay information, puzzle UI, and tutorial content. The main menu included an animated tutorial slideshow explaining interactions, hiding, item collection, puzzles, sanity, and journal usage.

Art Direction & Pixel Art Production

I took ownership of the visual pipeline during the final month when existing completed art was insufficient to support the game. I reprioritized my workload and produced approximately 90% of the final artwork, creating almost every visual asset outside the cutscene artwork and picture-puzzle source image.

  • Environment sprites, props, interactable objects, puzzle assets, and environmental details
  • UI, menus, journal artwork, icons, and gameplay feedback
  • Character-related sprites, animation assets, and supporting visual effects

Most spritesheets used approximately 64 × 64 pixel assets, with higher resolutions for animation panels and presentation elements. I also created the game’s animations. The picture-puzzle source image was separately produced by another teammate using generative AI, while cutscene assets were outside my primary art workload. I prioritized gameplay-critical assets so programming and level integration could continue.

2.5D Presentation

Although The Unraveling retained a top-down pixel-art presentation, GAM250 expanded NAgine beyond its purely 2D foundation. A layered environment system let objects appear in front of or behind the player based on height and collider positioning, creating depth as the player moved around furniture and props.

Particle System

Alongside gameplay development, I implemented a reusable engine-level particle system in C++. It was designed as a general NAgine feature, not something hardcoded specifically for the game.

Emitter properties exposed through the NAgine Inspector included play on start, looping, prewarm, duration, maximum particle count, spawn rate, start delay, local or world simulation, emission shape and radius, lifetime, speed, size, randomized angle, sprite, sprite layer, sort bias, starting rotation, size over lifetime, and starting and ending size.

For The Unraveling, I integrated particles into the interactable workflow so effects could identify relevant objects without relying entirely on conventional UI prompts. The workflow was inspired by Unity but implemented for our own engine.

Horror Pacing, Audio & Presentation

I designed how tension increased and released throughout the experience through lighting, layered background music, sound cues, spatial audio, camera shake, fog-of-war effects, sanity-based visual changes, scripted sequences, chase encounters, jumpscares, and environmental storytelling. I also designed major jumpscare scenes and their transitions into and out of gameplay.

Two teammates handled most audio implementation and content. FMOD spatial audio and layered sound supported the horror experience. Spatial audio, AI behaviour across scene transitions, and maintaining Kayo’s knowledge of the player’s previous position were significant collaborative technical challenges that influenced encounter and pacing design.

Playtesting & Iteration

Informal class playtesting informed substantial changes. We improved player movement responsiveness, simplified puzzles that created excessive cognitive pressure, reduced Kayo encounter frequency, gave her acceleration so players could react at chase start, and revised hiding so Kayo searched through line of sight rather than discovering players immediately. We also adjusted the balance of encounters, exploration, puzzles, and scripted scares to maintain tension without making every section equally intense.

Cross-Discipline Production

GAM250 was our first major experience across separate programming and design disciplines. Design readiness, documentation, implementation, and asset schedules had not been clarified early enough, creating communication and dependency problems once implementation began.

As Design Lead, I moved to implementation-focused communication and created or clarified puzzle user flows, success and failure states, player progression, interaction requirements, asset priorities, level requirements, and implementation priorities. The experience reinforced that design, art, and technical production cannot operate as isolated pipelines.

Production Challenges & Lessons

Alongside the game’s design direction, I was responsible for gameplay and engine programming, UI/UX, and most art production. During the final stretch, I spent a week of intensive production completing visual assets while preparing for other modules.

I prioritized a complete beginning-to-end game, clear puzzles, functional stealth, sanity management, stable enemy behaviour, player interactions, narrative clarity, complete environments, consistent presentation, reliable UI, and strong horror pacing. High-cost, lower-impact features were reduced or removed.

The project showed that capable technology alone is not enough: teams also need clear creative ownership, finalized requirements, reliable communication, and realistic priorities. In future, I would keep design and art ahead of technical implementation by locking gameplay, puzzle logic, progression, level flow, and asset requirements before programmers depend on them.

Project Outcome

The Unraveling received an A grade for GAM250 and received positive feedback at the final project presentation. Although the final demonstration encountered classroom lighting and technical issues, faculty noted the substantial improvement in visual quality, gameplay cohesion, design clarity, and completeness between the first and final milestones.

For me, the project demonstrated technical and production ownership. I entered intending to focus on implementation but ultimately established the design direction, restructured scope, produced most visual content, programmed major gameplay systems, and coordinated the design work needed to deliver the final experience.
