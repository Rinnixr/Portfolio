Overview

Shinwa was an immersive VR horror game developed as my Year 3 Semester 1 Capstone Project for Ngee Ann Polytechnic's Immersive Media course.

The game placed players inside a book of Japanese horror stories, with each chapter transporting them into a different self-contained scenario. Players could choose between two main levels inspired by Hachishakusama and Sadako, exploring each environment while completing puzzles and objectives needed to survive and escape the story.

Our goal was to create horror around atmosphere, environmental tension, player vulnerability, and immersion rather than relying entirely on jumpscares. VR allowed us to place the player directly inside each scenario, using spatial sound, environmental storytelling, lighting, visual distortion, enemy proximity, and scripted events to build tension.

I worked as the project's Game / Level Designer while also taking responsibility for project management and much of the game's implementation. I authored the initial proposal and Game Design Document, designed both levels and the overall player experience, built the levels in Unity, integrated all project assets, created gameplay and scripted-event logic, designed the interfaces, handled lighting and post-processing, produced animation and cinematics, modelled Hachishakusama, and contributed wherever production bottlenecks emerged.

The completed experience was designed for approximately 30 minutes of play, consisting of a tutorial and two levels of roughly 15 minutes each.

Project Design Goals

  • Create an immersive VR horror experience centred on Japanese horror stories and imagery.
  • Place the player directly inside each story rather than presenting the narrative from an external perspective.
  • Build fear through environmental tension, sound, pacing, lighting, visual effects, enemy behaviour, and scripted encounters.
  • Give players clear survival objectives while maintaining enough freedom to explore each environment.
  • Keep individual levels to approximately 15 minutes to reduce VR fatigue.
  • Support both standing and seated play.
  • Design interactions and visual information with lower-limb mobility and colour-vision accessibility in mind.
  • Develop the project's 2D and 3D assets internally to maintain a consistent visual identity.
  • Deliver a complete two-level VR experience within the scope of a four-person student team.

Game Design & Documentation

I authored the project's proposal and Game Design Document, defining the initial concept and establishing how the game would function before production began.

The documentation covered the core experience, story structure, gameplay, level progression, puzzles, player objectives, VR interaction requirements, environment design, accessibility considerations, UI/UX, horror pacing, and intended presentation.

I also contributed to the project's final README and supporting documentation.

Because I was responsible for both the design documentation and the implementation of the environments, I was able to carry the intended experience directly from concept into Unity and continually revise it as the project developed.

Narrative & Experience Design

The premise of Shinwa centred around a player who is transported into a book of stories they were reading. Each story became an explorable VR environment that the player had to survive before escaping.

The name Shinwa reflected this focus on stories and legends.

The completed project contained two main scenarios:

  • Hachishakusama - the first level.
  • Sadako - the second level.

Players were able to choose which story they wanted to enter rather than being required to complete them in a fixed sequence.

Within each level, the player explored the environment, solved puzzles, completed objectives, avoided or responded to threats, and worked toward escaping the story.

I developed the game's lore and narrative structure and co-wrote the narration with another team member.

Level Design

I was responsible for the complete level design of Shinwa, covering the tutorial and both main horror scenarios.

My work included:

  • Environment layouts
  • Player routes
  • Objective placement
  • Puzzle placement
  • Player flow
  • Horror pacing
  • Encounter placement
  • AI navigation and pathing
  • Environmental boundaries
  • Prop placement
  • Interaction placement
  • Navigation and progression through each scenario

Each environment gave players room to explore while remaining constrained to the location required by the story, such as the house or school.

The layouts needed to support both free exploration and controlled horror pacing. I therefore designed routes and encounters around when players were likely to reach particular spaces, while still allowing them to move through the environment without feeling as though they were following a completely linear corridor.

I also dressed every level in the game, placing environmental props and integrating completed assets to establish visual storytelling, guide player movement, and reinforce the atmosphere of each scenario.

Horror & Encounter Design

The horror experience was built using multiple overlapping systems rather than relying on a single type of scare.

I designed encounters using:

  • Environmental tension
  • Scripted events
  • Enemy encounters
  • Controlled pacing
  • Lighting changes
  • Visual distortion
  • Whispers and environmental sound
  • Spatial heartbeat feedback
  • Particle effects
  • Environmental storytelling

One system used a spatial heartbeat that became louder as the enemy approached the player. This communicated danger without requiring an explicit HUD warning while simultaneously increasing tension.

Post-processing effects applied to the player's camera were also used to distort the player's perception during particular moments.

These systems allowed the environment itself to communicate danger and helped maintain immersion within VR.

Puzzles & Player Objectives

Players progressed through each story by completing puzzles and objectives that allowed them to survive and eventually escape.

I designed how these objectives were positioned within each level and how players moved between them, balancing exploration with enough direction to prevent players from becoming lost.

The puzzle and objective structure also supported the horror pacing. Progression could be used to trigger environmental changes, scripted events, enemy encounters, or new areas rather than treating puzzles as isolated activities.

I implemented interaction and scripted-event logic in C#, connecting player actions to environmental responses and progression through the levels.

VR Locomotion & Player Comfort

The project used joystick-based locomotion rather than teleportation.

To reduce the amount of physical turning required, I retained snap rotation, allowing players to change orientation through the controllers rather than continually rotating their bodies in real space.

The game also included a standing / seated height toggle, allowing players to adjust the experience depending on whether they were playing while standing or sitting.

Interactive spaces and required actions were designed so that players with limited lower-limb mobility could still complete the experience.

Each main level was deliberately limited to approximately 15 minutes, resulting in roughly 30 minutes of total gameplay including the tutorial. This helped prevent physical and visual fatigue from overtaking the intended horror experience during longer VR sessions.

Accessibility Design

Accessibility considerations from our previous VR development work were carried forward and incorporated into Shinwa.

For players with colour-vision deficiencies, visual information and colour combinations were selected so important elements remained distinguishable without compromising the intended horror atmosphere.

For players who needed or preferred to remain seated, interactive areas were positioned within accessible reach and the game provided controller-based movement, snap rotation, and a dedicated seated-height option.

This was particularly important for a horror game because the experience relied on the player's ability to comfortably navigate and interact with the environment. Accessibility systems therefore needed to work alongside the atmosphere rather than making the player feel disconnected from the world.

UI / UX Design

I designed and integrated the player-facing interface throughout the project.

This included:

  • Tutorial interfaces
  • Interaction prompts
  • Instructions
  • UI positioning within VR
  • Player guidance
  • Supporting game-state information

The UI needed to remain readable without overwhelming the player's field of view or undermining the horror atmosphere.

I also handled a significant amount of GUI production and implementation when work fell outside the available capacity of the team's dedicated art disciplines.

Lighting, VFX & Post-Processing

I handled the lighting and visual-effects implementation across the project.

Lighting was used as both an atmospheric and gameplay tool, controlling visibility and helping direct the player's attention through dark environments.

I also created a flickering-light system, including the C# behaviour required to control it.

Additional visual work included:

  • Camera post-processing
  • Screen distortion
  • Particle systems
  • Environmental visual effects
  • Lighting changes for scripted sequences
  • Atmospheric effects used during encounters

These effects were designed alongside the level pacing so visual changes could reinforce particular moments rather than functioning purely as decoration.

Character Modelling & Animation

I modelled Hachishakusama in Autodesk Maya for use as one of the game's primary horror characters.

I was also responsible for animation throughout the entire game.

Mixamo animations were used as source material, but I edited and spliced multiple animations together to create sequences that better suited the characters, encounters, and scripted events required by the game.

This animation work extended beyond gameplay characters into the project's cinematic presentation.

Cinematics & Trailer Production

I created the game's cutscenes within Unity using character animation, environmental setup, and controlled camera movement.

I also produced the project's trailer video in Unity, using the same cinematic techniques to stage and capture sequences from the game.

This involved coordinating animation, cameras, character positioning, environment presentation, lighting, and timing to communicate the intended horror experience outside normal gameplay.

My implementation responsibilities included:

  • Importing and integrating all completed project assets
  • Building the level environments
  • Placing and dressing props
  • Configuring colliders
  • Setting gameplay boundaries
  • Setting up AI navigation and pathing
  • Integrating animations
  • Implementing UI
  • Setting up lighting
  • Creating particle effects
  • Applying post-processing
  • Implementing interactions
  • Scripting environmental and gameplay events
  • Creating cinematics

Because I handled the design, environment assembly, and integration together, I could continuously evaluate whether assets and systems supported the intended player experience once they were viewed inside VR.

Original Asset Production

The project's 2D and 3D visual assets were created specifically for Shinwa by the team rather than being built around premade visual asset packs.

The team's dedicated 3D artist produced the majority of the environment models, while the 2D artist produced the project's 2D artwork and supporting visual assets. I contributed additional production work where required, including Hachishakusama's model, UI/GUI work, animation, effects, environment assembly, and implementation.

Externally sourced content was limited primarily to background music and the Mixamo animation source material used as a basis for character animation.

Producing the visual assets internally allowed the environments, characters, UI, and overall atmosphere to follow the requirements established in the Game Design Document.

Production & Project Management

The project was developed by a four-person team:

  • Game / Level Designer - Myself
  • Programmer - Evan Talavera
  • 3D Artist - Muhammad Nur Syakir
  • 2D Artist - Jolie Ng

Alongside my design responsibilities, I managed the project's production and handled administrative, documentation, integration, and miscellaneous production work required to keep development moving.

The initial project scope proved too large for the size of the team.

During development, two team members also experienced personal circumstances that reduced their production capacity and created significant delays. Because much of my level-design and implementation work depended on completed art assets, delays earlier in the pipeline could prevent later work from continuing.

We ultimately restructured and reduced the project's scope so that the most important parts of the experience could be completed to a stronger standard.

To prevent production from stalling, I took on remaining tasks outside my primary discipline where necessary, particularly GUI work, implementation, animation, scripting, documentation, administrative work, and general backlog tasks. This allowed the other team members to concentrate on their primary disciplines while I continued assembling the final experience.

The project highlighted the importance of planning dependencies between disciplines. In retrospect, beginning 2D and 3D asset production approximately one to two weeks ahead of downstream implementation would have reduced bottlenecks and allowed level assembly and gameplay integration to progress more consistently in parallel.

Project Outcome

Shinwa received a Distinction for the final-year Capstone Project and was recognised as the top project.

The final experience delivered a tutorial and two complete VR horror scenarios within a roughly 30-minute playtime despite the project's original scope having to be reduced during production.

The project became an exercise not only in VR game and level design, but also in adapting a production plan under pressure: identifying which parts of the original design were essential, restructuring scope around the team's available capacity, and contributing across disciplines to ensure those core elements reached completion.
