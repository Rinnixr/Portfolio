Conquest

A single-player positional strategy board game where players manage territory, movement, resources, and formations while predicting an Assassin's route across the kingdom.

Overview

Conquest was a single-player positional strategy board game developed for Introduction to Game Design during my first year at DigiPen Singapore.

The player controls a Prince being hunted by an Assassin across a medieval kingdom. Over a maximum of eight in-game days, the player must travel between territories, establish control over the map, gather supplies, improve allied characters, and prepare a favorable combat position before confronting the Assassin.

The central design goal was to make positioning matter several turns before combat actually occurred.

Rather than treating the board as a space players simply moved across, I designed the map, enemy movement, territory economy, and formation systems to continuously influence one another. Where the player chose to travel affected the resources they could generate, where their allies could be positioned, how quickly the Assassin could reach them, and what combat options would be available later.

I worked as the project's Game Designer and Art Lead. I originated the core concept, designed the complete 29-territory map and node network, developed the territory and Point of Interest systems, designed the Event cards, balanced movement and resources, and collaborated closely with another design lead on combat, character mechanics, and the overall gameplay loop.

I also led physical prototyping and playtesting, wrote and designed the complete rulebook, assembled the Print and Play package, and produced approximately 50% of the final artwork, including the entire illustrated game board.

The project received an A grade and was among the strongest projects in the class.

Design Goals

  • Make forward planning and positional decision-making the primary source of strategy.
  • Give players enough information about the Assassin to predict its movement and plan around it.
  • Design a map where routes had different strategic values rather than one optimal path.
  • Connect territory control directly to resource generation.
  • Make formations reward preparation that began several turns before combat.
  • Create competing demands for limited time and resources so the player could never comfortably maximize everything.
  • Use the eight-day limit to force players to decide when they were prepared enough to confront the Assassin.
  • Create a more mechanically interconnected strategy game than our previous physical project, Acquit.

Designing for Positional Strategy

The main design question behind Conquest was:

How can decisions made now determine what options the player has several turns later?

This influenced nearly every system.

The player could see where the Assassin was and understand how it was likely to move. This meant the challenge was not simply reacting when the enemy appeared.

Instead, players needed to think ahead: Where will the Assassin be in several phases? Which route gives me enough time to prepare? Should I take a longer path for a valuable Point of Interest? Which territories are worth conquering if the Assassin may pass through them later? Where should my allies move now so they can participate in combat later? Should I spend resources healing or invest them into a stronger future formation? When should I stop expanding and prepare for the final fight?

The intended experience was built around prediction followed by commitment. Players could make informed plans, but every route or investment came with an opportunity cost.

Map Design & Route Planning

I designed the complete 29-territory map, including its four regions, node connections, road network, choke points, Strongholds, region transitions, and Points of Interest.

The map was designed mechanically before it was treated as an illustration.

Every connection affected movement efficiency, Assassin routing, access to resources, territory income, ally positioning, formation setup, access to special locations, and recovery from poor routing decisions.

I wanted different routes to create different trade-offs. A direct route might save Action Points but provide fewer opportunities to gather resources. A longer route could offer access to healing or other Points of Interest, but delay the player enough for the Assassin to close the distance.

This meant a route was never valuable purely because it was short. Its value depended on the player's current situation.

Route Value = Movement Cost + Resources + Assassin Position + Ally Position + Remaining Time

I also deliberately designed the board so its underlying structure could survive balancing changes. The regions contained comparable choke points, alternate routes, and strategic intersections. If a Point of Interest proved too powerful in one location during testing, we could move that effect to another compatible node rather than redesigning the entire map.

This allowed the board itself to remain stable while the systems placed on top of it continued to evolve.

Action Points & Movement Economy

Movement uses an Action Point system, designed collaboratively between the other design lead and me after being inspired by the action economy in Divinity: Original Sin 2.

I established and balanced the travel costs between routes. Different connections required different amounts of AP, which allowed geography to become part of the strategy.

A destination could appear close on the board while still representing a significant commitment if the route required more Action Points. This made movement a resource rather than a free transition between locations.

Players needed to decide whether spending extra AP was justified by a stronger Point of Interest, better future positioning, additional territory, reaching an ally, escaping the Assassin's expected route, or creating a better combat setup.

The player was constantly trading time for position.

Designing the Assassin

The Assassin was intentionally designed as a predictable threat rather than a random one.

The enemy moves toward the Prince based on the map's route structure, with its movement changing according to the current phase of the day. This gave the player enough information to estimate where the Assassin would be in future phases.

That predictability was essential to the game. If the enemy moved randomly, careful route planning and formation setup could be invalidated by chance. If the Assassin was completely avoidable, there would be little reason to engage with the pressure systems at all.

The challenge was therefore finding the right balance: predictable enough to plan around, fast enough to remain threatening.

The Assassin effectively became a moving deadline on the board. The player might know exactly where it was heading, but still need to decide whether to continue toward a valuable location, reposition an ally, retreat toward safety, sacrifice control of a territory, allow the Assassin to approach intentionally, or begin preparing for combat.

This turned enemy movement into one of the game's main strategic systems rather than simply an AI procedure.

Territory, Resources & Route Value

I designed the game's territorial-control system and connected it directly to the resource economy.

The Prince can conquer territories while travelling through the map. Controlled territories generate supplies, making expansion the primary way to improve the player's economy.

However, control is not permanent. The Assassin can pass through conquered territories and remove the player's control.

This meant players could not simply expand outward and assume every captured territory would remain useful. They needed to consider whether a territory was easy to maintain, likely to be crossed by the Assassin, worth the movement required to reach it, useful for future routes, and valuable enough to justify defending.

Territory ownership therefore became another form of prediction. A player could technically conquer a location, but that did not necessarily mean they should.

Resource Balancing

Supplies could be spent on areas such as healing and character improvements.

My balancing goal was:

The player should become stronger over time, but never have enough time or resources to comfortably buy everything.

If resources were too generous, there was little reason to make difficult territorial choices. If resources were too limited, players could not meaningfully engage with upgrades and formations.

The economy therefore needed to create competing priorities: heal now or save for an upgrade, capture another territory or prepare for combat, improve an ally or preserve resources for the Prince, and take a longer route for greater income or choose the safer path.

The fixed eight-day deadline reinforced this. Every additional turn spent building the economy was also another turn in which the Assassin continued moving.

Points of Interest

I proposed and designed the Point of Interest system to give individual locations value beyond normal territory control.

Points of Interest included locations such as Strongholds, Camps, Watchtowers, Fishing Ports, and other special spaces that could provide healing, resources, regional effects, or other strategic benefits.

Their purpose was to create strategic temptation. A Point of Interest could offer a powerful advantage, but reaching it might require the player to spend more Action Points, move away from an ally, enter the Assassin's route, delay another objective, or sacrifice a stronger long-term position.

This made parts of the map valuable for different reasons. The player was not simply deciding where to go. They were deciding what advantage was worth changing their route for.

Formations & Combat

Combat was designed collaboratively between the other design lead and me, with another teammate initially proposing the concept of formations.

The formation system became one of the clearest ways to reward long-term positional planning.

Friendly characters included the Prince, Vanguard Captain, and Shield Warden, with each contributing differently to combat. Rather than allowing every ally to contribute regardless of location, characters needed to be positioned appropriately on the map.

This meant combat began several turns before the actual encounter. If the player wanted a particular formation, they first needed to consider where the Assassin was likely to arrive, which allies were required, how many turns those allies needed to reach the location, whether the Prince could reach the same area, whether the necessary routes were affordable, and whether preparing the formation was worth delaying another objective.

Formations such as Retribution and Preservation then rewarded those earlier decisions with different combat advantages.

Prediction → Positioning → Formation → Combat Payoff

Positional Risk

The Assassin's own attacks also changed depending on how many player-controlled units were involved. This prevented the optimal strategy from simply being placing every ally beside the enemy.

More participating characters could increase the player's combat potential, but could also expose the group to stronger enemy responses. The player therefore needed to think about both sides of the positioning problem.

It was not enough to ask, "How do I maximize my formation?" They also needed to consider, "What does this position allow the Assassin to do?"

Event Cards

I designed the game's Event cards to introduce variation without overriding the player's strategic planning.

Events could affect allies, the Assassin, regions, movement, combat, resources, and conquering. The purpose was not to randomly invalidate the player's strategy. Instead, Events temporarily changed the conditions under which that strategy had to operate.

For example, a route that was normally efficient might become less attractive for a turn, requiring the player to reconsider their immediate plan while still working toward the same longer-term goal.

This helped prevent repeated playthroughs from following identical routes without turning Conquest into a primarily luck-driven game.

Playtesting & Balancing

We conducted more than 20 playtests using friends, family, team members, and players with different levels of gaming experience.

The three systems that required the most balancing were Assassin movement, resource generation, and Prince survivability. These systems collectively determined how much pressure the player experienced.

Assassin Movement: If the Assassin moved too quickly, players could not establish territory or prepare formations before being forced into combat. If it moved too slowly, the player could build an overwhelming advantage before the enemy became threatening.

Resources: Too many supplies made upgrades automatic and weakened territorial decision-making. Too few supplies made some of the more interesting character and formation strategies inaccessible.

Prince Survivability: If the Prince was too fragile, one poor movement decision could decide the entire game. If the Prince was too durable, encounters stopped carrying meaningful risk.

The important lesson was that these values could not be balanced independently. Changing Assassin speed also changed how many turns the player had to generate resources. Changing resources affected how quickly characters could become stronger. Changing survivability altered how aggressively players were willing to route near the Assassin.

This became one of the strongest systems-design exercises in the project.

Art Direction & Physical Production

I served as Art Lead and created approximately 50% of the final visual assets.

The visual direction was inspired by medieval aesthetics and animated fantasy, particularly Disenchantment.

The final game used parchment textures, heraldic imagery, medieval cartography, decorative borders, shields and swords, compass motifs, regional colour identities, and illustrated terrain.

No AI-generated artwork was used. All final assets were created by the team, although some character designs used strong visual references during development.

Board Illustration

I illustrated the entire game board from scratch.

Because I was also responsible for its mechanical design, I could make the visual hierarchy support gameplay directly.

I created terrain, regional identities, forests, mountains, rivers, settlements, Strongholds, roads, territory numbering, Point of Interest markers, regional boundaries, compass elements, and the map legend.

The challenge was making the board function as both an illustration and an interface. Routes needed to remain immediately readable. Regions needed to be identifiable so regional Event effects were easy to resolve. Territory numbers and Points of Interest needed to stand out without overwhelming the illustrated terrain.

Rulebook & Player Onboarding

I wrote and designed the complete 16-page rulebook.

Because Conquest was significantly more complex than our previous card game, communicating the design clearly became a major part of the project's UX work.

The rulebook covered components, board structure, setup, characters, turn flow, movement, conquering, combat, Assassin behaviour, upgrades, formations, Points of Interest, Events, and basic strategy.

I also created diagrams and visual examples where the systems were easier to understand spatially than through text alone.

We tested the final documentation with players of different ages and gaming familiarity. Players with and without gaming experience were able to learn, set up, and play the game without requiring continuous explanation from our team.

For a systems-heavy physical game, I considered that an important validation of both the rules and the interface design.

Print & Play

I assembled the final Print and Play package, including the board and supporting physical components.

The package contained elements such as board sections, Event cards, formation references, character standees, character sheets, counters, tokens, and supporting gameplay components.

Preparing the Print and Play version required considering physical usability alongside the game rules, including component size, readability, assembly, table space, and how frequently information needed to be referenced during play.

Project Outcome

Conquest received an A grade and was among the strongest projects in the class.

A complete game generally lasted between 30 minutes and one hour, depending on how quickly the player evaluated their movement and resource decisions.

For me, the most important outcome was learning how to create strategy through the relationship between systems rather than through the complexity of any single mechanic.

The central design relationship became:

Map Layout → Movement Cost → Position → Territory → Resources → Upgrades → Formations → Assassin Movement → Combat

The map determined which choices were available. Movement costs determined which choices were affordable. Territory determined economic strength. Resources determined which future advantages could be purchased. Formations rewarded earlier positioning. The Assassin applied pressure to every stage of that process.

Together, these systems produced the intended experience: observe the board, predict what will happen next, and deliberately create the conditions for a successful confrontation.
